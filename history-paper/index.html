II · The story

Read the lesson

Rome invaded Britain in A.D. forty-three, and an exiled ruler gave it the excuse. Tradition remembers a king of the Atrebates named Verica, and the coins say rex. When the powerful leader Cunobelinus died and his son Caratacus rose, Verica was driven out, and fled to the emperor Claudius, in Rome. And Rome listened. Now Doctor Miles Russell, an archaeologist at Bournemouth University, argues we've read one word wrong. Rex, he says, didn't have to mean a man, though critics note Rome never clearly called a queen rex. Latin names ending in a often belong to women. And some of Verica's coins appear to show a bare-breasted rider, unlike the clearly male riders on Cunobelinus's and Caratacus's coins. Not everyone is convinced. So, king or queen? Your verdict.

Sources

Every factual line in the lesson was checked against the sources below before a single paper cut was drawn. The claim is presented as Dr Russell's argument, with the named doubters — exactly as the reporting does.

Deliberately not claimed: the lesson never says Rome's historians mocked Claudius over the affair, and never says Verica "was called a king for 200 years" — both lines circulate but were not part of the checked reporting.

The coin shown in the lesson is an illustrative paper-cut marked VERICA · REX — not a reproduction of a real Verica coin.
